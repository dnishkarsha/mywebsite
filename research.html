Understanding earthquake shaking from the ground up

Local soil and rock conditions can amplify or reduce earthquake shaking. My work asks when simple one-dimensional models are good enough, and builds the multi-dimensional models and field datasets needed when they are not.

01 · The problem

Why the last few tens of meters matter

During the 1985 M8 Mexico City earthquake, roughly 10,000 people died and more than 400 buildings collapsed, largely because of local site effects. The soft soils beneath the city make up only about 0.01% of the ~400 km path the seismic waves traveled, yet they dramatically amplified the shaking.

Engineers usually predict this amplification with one-dimensional (1D) site response analyses. Downhole arrays, which record earthquakes both at the surface and at depth, let us check those predictions against reality, and the record is sobering: about half of 662 downhole array sites are poorly modeled by 1D analyses (Hallal et al. 2022). At many sites the theoretical transfer functions (TTFs) systematically over-predict the empirical ones (ETFs) computed from recorded motions.

My dissertation asks why, and whether moving to 2D and 3D models, better damping formulations, inclined waves, or spatial variability can close the gap.

02 · Study sites

Four downhole arrays, four site-specific 3D Vs models

For each array I built a pseudo-3D shear-wave velocity (Vs) model by combining spatially distributed horizontal-to-vertical spectral ratio (H/V) measurements with geostatistical kriging. From each 3D model, the most laterally variable 2D cross-section (for example, azimuth 165° at Delaney Park) is extracted for 2D analyses, and km-scale 2D and 3D models are run in FLAC.

03 · Damping formulations

How should numerical models dissipate energy?

Even 2D and 3D analyses on site-specific models still over-predicted the recorded amplification peak. That pointed to damping: real soil loses energy not only through intrinsic material damping but through “apparent” damping (wave scattering and mode conversion) that the models do not capture.

I back-calculated the minimum damping (Dmin) multipliers needed to match the recordings: 8× at DPDA, 10× at I15DA, 2× at TIDA and 3× at GVDA. The multiplier correlates with velocity contrast, so low-contrast sites need larger multipliers.

I then compared three formulations: Full Rayleigh (FR), Maxwell (MX) and Rayleigh mass-only (RM). Rayleigh mass-only performed best at three of four sites, with 27–44% lower transfer-function misfit than Full Rayleigh and 44–49% lower than Maxwell. It also allowed 5–30× larger time steps (at DPDA, FR drops to 0.032 ms while RM stays at 0.35 ms). Its frequency-dependent damping decays much like the trend Kokusho (2017) observed across KiK-net sites.

04 · Inclined incident waves

Does the angle of the incoming wave explain the mismatch?

Most site response models assume waves arrive vertically. I tested two ways of applying inclined waves in km-scale 2D and 3D FLAC models: the Input Lag Method (ILM) and the Inclined Domain Method (IDM). IDM needs up to ~500% more zones at 30°, and ILM performed better, so it was used throughout.

  • Up to 15° inclination, transfer-function misfit changed by less than 13% (2D: 1.88 → 1.65; 3D: 1.68 → 1.62).
  • At 60°, misfit grew to 3.19 (2D) and 3.23 (3D), worse than 1D (2.35), because steep angles shift peaks to frequencies not seen in the recordings.
  • The best 3D result at DPDA came at +30° (r = 0.82, mTF = 1.52) versus 1D (r = 0.66, mTF = 2.35).
  • Azimuth is a secondary effect (mTF 1.28–1.55 across four azimuths at 15°).

Bottom line: wave inclination improves the fit modestly but does not explain the low recorded peak.

05 · The downhole array conundrum manuscript in preparation

Part of the gap lives at the bottom of the borehole

A downhole sensor records both the upgoing wave and the downgoing wave reflected from the surface, about 0.36 s apart at DPDA. The downgoing wave carves a “spectral hole” in the transfer function at the site frequency. In the models that hole is about twice as deep as recorded, which is why model peaks reach ~25–30 versus 5.6 in the data. Removing the downgoing wave brings 1D transfer-function peaks close to the recorded ones at all four arrays.

To test whether realistic variability could fill the gap, I ran:

  • 2D Vs random fields (σlnVs 0.1 and 0.3; correlation lengths of 12×6 m and 100×10 m), 120 runs plus a 40-run pool
  • 3D random fields (15 realizations)
  • Incoherent input motions using random lags and the SVGM coherency model (Abrahamson 2006) at 15°
  • Combined soil and input variability across 30 site-consistent soil models

06 · Takeaways & future work

What this means for practice

  • Multi-dimensional site response alone still over-predicts amplification at downhole arrays.
  • In practice, calibrated Dmin multipliers combined with Rayleigh mass-only damping give the best results and run faster.
  • Part of the mismatch comes from the downhole “within” boundary condition itself, not only from the soil model.

Next steps

  • Extend the multiplier vs. velocity-contrast relationship to more arrays
  • Couple regional wavefield simulations to the local site models
  • Measure in-situ damping that separates intrinsic from apparent damping (as in Aimar et al. 2026)
  • Run nonlinear 2D and 3D analyses for stronger shaking

Geophysical site characterization

Field data are at the heart of my work. I develop deep shear-wave velocity profiles (to roughly 1 km) using active- and passive-source surface-wave methods (MASW and MAM), including NQA-1-compliant studies for nuclear power facilities in Washington, Michigan, and Wyoming.

I also helped run a large subsurface imaging experiment in Newberry, Florida, using about 2 km of DAS cable alongside nodal seismometers, and the resulting datasets are openly available on DesignSafe.
